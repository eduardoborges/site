In 2023 it'll be 10 years since I shipped a serious project that actually impacted an organization. In that time I learned lessons that were sometimes hard to accept, others confirmed fears I already had. But what they all have in common is that they made me not just a better software engineer, but a better person.

This post doesn't have a clear goal, I could just call it a rant.

## People: the most important element

I often heard comments like: "must be nice not having to deal with people, just sitting there fighting with the machine." You'll rarely ship software that meaningfully impacts an organization and/or a large group of users all by yourself.

Over time you start valuing a good relationship with your colleagues more, and noticing how much it improves the whole team's performance.

## Listen more

It's not just about hearing your coworker's technical opinion, **it's actually testing the ideas put on the table**. No matter how senior you are, you're not always right. Learning to swallow your ego and make room for other ideas is essential, and a hell of a soft skill.

## You don't know everything

And you never will. Everything is so volatile, so fast. You can't keep up. Having the simplicity to raise your hand and say *"hey, I don't know how to do this, can someone help me?"* was something I used to be ashamed of for a long time. Over time I came to understand that's part of the job, and normalized it. The same goes for helping others.

## Everything is political, especially software

Software is made by... guess who? That's right, **people**. The influence of their beliefs and values can show up in the organization and/or project, including political issues. Selling data, decision-making algorithms, negative impacts on society in all sorts of ways. You have to be aligned with the company and project you work for. Also, decision algorithms used in many fields, like credit, employment and healthcare, can reinforce existing inequalities in society when trained on historically incomplete and limited data. After all, are you going to close your eyes to the impact your work has on society?

And I'm not even going to get into open source software... that's for another post. Deserves a full one on its own.

## Understand your privilege

Have you ever looked around and only seen white men? Yeah, sit with that for a second. Computing is an extremely sexist and anti-diversity field. Ironic, honestly, given who the actual founders of computing were. I've seen reports of candidates sending out 2 versions of their resume, one with a male name and one with a female name, and guess what? 80% more interview invites for the male version. The exact same resume.

And who do you think performs better, the buddy who doesn't even have to make his own lunch, or the buddy who cleans the house, takes a 2 hour bus ride to work every day, and cooks?

The point here isn't to say who's right or wrong. Or to offer solutions. **It's knowing these problems exist, and that changes everything.**

## Take care of your body and mind

Better yet, taking care of one directly impacts the other. Exercising is essential for us computer folks, especially for those who spend most of the day sitting.

It boosts your wellbeing and really helps with anxiety. A simple walk around your neighborhood or the gym is enough. Try to make it a routine. Our tendency toward obesity in this field is pretty significant.

## Feedback is essential

The negative ones are my favorite. I'm human, I'm flawed. I see negative feedback as a way to become better for myself and my team. Of course, that requires knowing how to handle it, not getting discouraged, and staying positive about the change.

Of course, how the feedback reaches you matters a lot, but how you receive it matters too.
